# Vendor Performance Management

## Purpose

Vendors play an important role in workplace operations, but vendor management does not end once a contract is signed or a service request is submitted.

This guide outlines a practical approach for evaluating vendor performance, documenting service issues, reviewing costs, communicating expectations, and deciding when additional action is needed.

## Common Workplace Vendors

Workplace Operations may work with vendors providing:

* Janitorial services
* Food and beverage services
* Catering
* Office supplies
* Furniture
* Security systems
* Building maintenance
* HVAC services
* Moving services
* Shipping and courier services
* Pest control
* Audio visual support
* Equipment maintenance

## 1. Define Expectations

Vendor performance is easier to evaluate when expectations are clear.

Depending on the service, expectations may include:

* Service frequency
* Response time
* Completion time
* Quality standards
* Delivery schedule
* Communication requirements
* Pricing
* Invoice accuracy
* Escalation procedures

When possible, these expectations should align with the vendor agreement, contract, or service level requirements.

## 2. Track Vendor Information

Maintain useful information for each vendor.

Example:

| Field | Information |
| --- | --- |
| Vendor | Example Facilities Services |
| Service | Office maintenance |
| Primary Contact | Vendor Account Manager |
| Internal Owner | Workplace Operations |
| Service Frequency | As Needed |
| Billing Cycle | Monthly |
| Contract Renewal | January |
| Escalation Contact | Regional Manager |

Keeping this information organized makes routine coordination and escalation easier.

## 3. Monitor Service Quality

Vendor performance should be evaluated based on actual service.

Consider:

* Was the work completed?
* Was the work completed correctly?
* Did the vendor arrive when expected?
* Were employees disrupted unnecessarily?
* Did the issue return?
* Was the worksite left clean and safe?
* Did the vendor communicate delays?

A completed work order does not always mean the service met expectations.

## 4. Track Recurring Problems

Document repeated issues.

Examples may include:

* Late deliveries
* Missed service appointments
* Incomplete repairs
* Incorrect orders
* Poor cleaning quality
* Slow response times
* Billing errors
* Lack of communication
* Repeated equipment failures

Tracking patterns helps distinguish an isolated mistake from an ongoing performance issue.

## 5. Review Response Times

For service requests, track:

* Time request was submitted
* Time vendor acknowledged request
* Scheduled service date
* Actual arrival time
* Completion time

For urgent workplace issues, response time can significantly affect business operations.

## 6. Verify Invoices

Before approving or routing an invoice, confirm:

* Service was actually provided
* Dates are correct
* Pricing matches expectations
* Quantities are accurate
* Additional fees are explained
* Credits are applied when appropriate

Unexpected charges should be reviewed before payment.

## 7. Communicate Performance Issues

When addressing an issue with a vendor, provide specific information.

Instead of:

"Service has been bad lately."

Use:

"The scheduled kitchen service was missed on September 3 and again on September 10. Both incidents required Workplace Operations to contact the vendor after the service window had passed."

Specific examples make the discussion easier to resolve.

## 8. Create a Corrective Action Plan

For recurring issues, agree on what needs to change.

A corrective plan may include:

* Clear performance expectation
* Specific action the vendor will take
* Responsible vendor contact
* Deadline
* Follow up date
* Measurement of improvement

Document the agreement.

## 9. Escalate When Necessary

Escalation may be appropriate when:

* The same issue continues after feedback
* The vendor does not respond
* Service failures create safety concerns
* Business operations are repeatedly disrupted
* Contract requirements are not being met
* Billing problems continue
* Leadership involvement is required

Escalation should include documentation of previous attempts to resolve the problem.

## 10. Review Vendor Value

Vendor performance should be considered in relation to overall value.

Factors may include:

* Cost
* Service quality
* Reliability
* Responsiveness
* Employee experience
* Vendor expertise
* Administrative effort required
* Ability to scale with workplace needs

The cheapest vendor may not provide the best value if frequent problems require significant employee time to manage.

## 11. Contract and Renewal Review

Before a vendor agreement renews, review:

* Performance history
* Pricing
* Service utilization
* Recurring problems
* Employee feedback
* Invoice accuracy
* Available alternatives
* Future workplace needs

This review can help determine whether the service should continue, change, or be competitively evaluated.

## Example

**Situation:** A workplace service vendor repeatedly missed scheduled service windows.

**Review:** Documented missed visits, employee impact, follow up communication, and the vendor's responses.

**Action taken:** Reviewed the performance concerns with the vendor account manager, clarified expected service times, and established a follow up period.

**Result:** Service reliability improved and future missed visits had a clear escalation process.

## Key Takeaway

Effective vendor management requires more than maintaining contact information.

Strong vendor relationships depend on clear expectations, performance tracking, accurate documentation, cost awareness, and timely follow up when service does not meet workplace needs.
