# Food and Beverage Program Management

## Purpose

A workplace food and beverage program should support employee experience while remaining organized, inclusive, cost conscious, and easy to maintain.

This guide outlines a practical process for managing snacks, beverages, catering, vendors, dietary needs, inventory, and recurring workplace food programs.

## Program Goals

A strong program should:

* Provide reliable options for employees
* Support a variety of dietary needs
* Maintain appropriate inventory levels
* Control unnecessary spending
* Reduce food waste
* Keep kitchens and shared areas organized
* Make vendor performance easy to evaluate

## 1. Understand Employee Needs

Before building or changing a program, understand how employees actually use it.

Consider:

* Office population
* Onsite attendance patterns
* Popular snacks and beverages
* Dietary restrictions
* Allergies
* Cultural preferences
* Remote, hybrid, or onsite schedules
* Event and meeting needs

Employee feedback can help identify what is being used and what is being wasted.

## 2. Establish Program Categories

Common categories may include:

* Coffee and tea
* Cold beverages
* Snacks
* Breakfast items
* Fresh fruit
* Dairy and nondairy options
* Meeting catering
* Event catering
* Kitchen supplies

Organizing purchases into categories makes ordering and budget tracking easier.

## 3. Dietary Considerations

Provide reasonable options for common dietary needs when possible.

These may include:

* Vegetarian
* Vegan
* Gluten free
* Dairy free
* Nut free
* Low sugar options

Avoid making assumptions about an employee's dietary needs.

For catered events, collect requirements through an appropriate process and communicate them clearly to the vendor.

## 4. Vendor Selection

When evaluating vendors, consider:

* Product quality
* Pricing
* Delivery reliability
* Available dietary options
* Minimum order requirements
* Delivery fees
* Customer service
* Billing process
* Sustainability practices when relevant

The lowest cost vendor is not always the best choice if deliveries are frequently late or orders are inaccurate.

## 5. Inventory Management

Check food and beverage inventory on a regular schedule.

Monitor:

* Current quantities
* High use items
* Low use items
* Expiration dates
* Storage space
* Upcoming events
* Expected office attendance

Set reorder points for frequently used items.

## 6. Ordering Process

Before placing an order:

1. Review current inventory.
2. Check expected office attendance.
3. Review upcoming meetings or events.
4. Compare the order with recent usage.
5. Confirm pricing.
6. Follow purchasing approval procedures.
7. Record the order and expected delivery date.

Avoid ordering based only on habit.

## 7. Receiving Deliveries

When deliveries arrive:

* Confirm the order is complete
* Check quantities
* Inspect damaged or missing items
* Store products appropriately
* Rotate older products forward
* Record vendor problems when necessary

Temperature sensitive items should be stored promptly.

## 8. Kitchen Organization

A well organized kitchen reduces waste and makes inventory easier to manage.

Useful practices include:

* Clearly labeled storage
* Consistent product locations
* Regular refrigerator checks
* Organized cabinets
* Visible waste and recycling stations
* Easy access to commonly used items

Do not overfill storage areas simply because space is available.

## 9. Catering Coordination

For meetings and events, confirm:

* Attendance
* Budget
* Dietary requirements
* Delivery time
* Setup location
* Serving supplies
* Cleanup plan
* Vendor contact information

Build in reasonable delivery time before important meetings.

## 10. Cost Management

Monitor recurring food and beverage spending.

Possible cost controls include:

* Adjusting quantities based on attendance
* Consolidating orders
* Reviewing underused items
* Comparing vendor pricing
* Reducing unnecessary delivery fees
* Avoiding excessive event ordering
* Reviewing recurring subscriptions

Cost reduction should not create unnecessary operational problems or eliminate useful employee options without understanding usage first.

## 11. Waste Reduction

Food waste may indicate that ordering levels need adjustment.

Track patterns such as:

* Repeatedly discarded products
* Catering leftovers
* Expired items
* Low use beverages
* Large differences between expected and actual attendance

Use those patterns to improve future ordering.

## 12. Employee Feedback

Periodic feedback can help improve the program.

Useful questions include:

* Which items are most useful?
* Which items are rarely used?
* Are important dietary options missing?
* Are there recurring kitchen issues?
* Are employees satisfied with available choices?

Feedback should support decisions rather than automatically result in every requested item being added.

## 13. Vendor Performance

Track recurring vendor issues such as:

* Late deliveries
* Missing products
* Incorrect orders
* Damaged items
* Billing errors
* Poor communication

Repeated issues should be documented and reviewed before renewing or expanding a vendor relationship.

## Example

**Situation:** The office was regularly throwing away unused snacks while several popular items continued to run out.

**Review:** Compared ordering quantities with actual consumption and employee feedback.

**Action taken:** Reduced orders for low use products, increased reorder points for high demand items, and adjusted ordering frequency.

**Result:** Inventory became more predictable, employees had better access to preferred items, and unnecessary waste was reduced.

## Key Takeaway

A successful food and beverage program balances employee experience with organization, cost awareness, dietary needs, vendor reliability, and waste reduction.

The strongest programs use actual consumption and employee feedback to guide decisions rather than relying on fixed ordering habits.
