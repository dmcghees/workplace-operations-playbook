# Standard Operating Procedure Creation

## Purpose

Standard Operating Procedures help turn recurring work into clear, repeatable processes.

A strong SOP explains what needs to be done, who is responsible, when the process should be used, and how the work should be completed.

The goal is to make important workplace processes easier to follow, train, maintain, and improve.

## When an SOP Is Useful

An SOP may be useful when:

* A task happens regularly
* Multiple people perform the same process
* Training takes too long because instructions are informal
* Important steps are being missed
* A process involves several teams or vendors
* Responsibilities are unclear
* A task has compliance, safety, or security requirements
* A process needs to continue when the usual owner is unavailable

## SOP Development Process

### 1. Identify the Process

Start by clearly defining what the SOP will cover.

Avoid making the scope too broad.

For example:

Instead of:

"Office Operations"

Use:

"Weekly Office Supply Inventory and Reordering"

A focused SOP is easier to understand and maintain.

### 2. Define the Purpose

Explain why the process exists.

A useful purpose statement should answer:

* What does this process accomplish?
* Why is it important?
* What problem does it prevent or solve?

### 3. Identify the Owner

Determine who is responsible for the process.

This may include:

* Process owner
* Primary person responsible
* Backup owner
* Approver
* Supporting teams

Clear ownership helps prevent tasks from being missed.

### 4. Gather Information

Before writing the procedure, understand how the work is actually performed.

Information may come from:

* Performing the process directly
* Observing another employee
* Reviewing existing documentation
* Speaking with process owners
* Reviewing vendor requirements
* Reviewing company policies
* Identifying common problems or exceptions

The written SOP should reflect the real process rather than an idealized version that employees do not actually use.

### 5. Document the Steps

Write the process in the order it should be completed.

Use clear, direct instructions.

For example:

1. Review current inventory levels.
2. Compare quantities with established reorder points.
3. Identify items that require replenishment.
4. Confirm approved vendor and pricing.
5. Submit the order according to purchasing procedures.
6. Record the order date and expected delivery date.

Each step should describe a specific action.

### 6. Include Exceptions

Not every situation will follow the standard process.

Document common exceptions when they are important.

Examples may include:

* Vendor unavailable
* Urgent request
* Required approval is unavailable
* System outage
* Item is out of stock
* Security or safety concern

Explain when the employee should stop the normal process and escalate the issue.

### 7. Add Supporting Information

An SOP may include:

* Checklists
* Screenshots
* Forms
* Templates
* Contact information
* System names
* Vendor information
* Related policies
* Escalation procedures

Only include information that helps someone complete the process correctly.

## Testing the SOP

Before publishing the procedure, have someone other than the author test it when possible.

Ask them to follow the instructions without additional explanation.

Look for:

* Missing steps
* Unclear wording
* Incorrect assumptions
* Missing access requirements
* Steps that are out of order
* Information that is no longer accurate

Update the SOP based on what is discovered.

## Version Control

Important procedures should include basic version information.

Example:

| Field | Information |
| --- | --- |
| Process Owner | Workplace Operations |
| Version | 1.0 |
| Effective Date | September 2026 |
| Last Reviewed | September 2026 |
| Next Review | March 2027 |

Version control helps employees determine whether they are using the current procedure.

## Review and Maintenance

An SOP should be reviewed when:

* The process changes
* A new system is introduced
* A vendor changes
* Responsibilities change
* Employees repeatedly encounter the same problem
* A policy or compliance requirement changes

Even stable procedures should be reviewed periodically.

## Example

**Process:** New employee office setup

**Problem:** Workplace setup tasks were being coordinated through separate emails, making it difficult to confirm whether everything had been completed.

**Approach:** Documented the required steps, assigned responsibility for each item, created a checklist, and defined when requests should be submitted.

**Result:** The process became easier to track, responsibilities were clearer, and incomplete setup items could be identified before the employee's first day.

## Key Takeaway

A useful SOP should make a process easier to perform without relying on institutional knowledge.

The best procedures are clear, practical, easy to maintain, and detailed enough for another person to complete the work successfully.
