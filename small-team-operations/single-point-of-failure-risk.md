# Single Point of Failure Risk

## Purpose

Small teams often rely heavily on individual employees.

That can make the organization fast and flexible, but it can also create operational risk when critical knowledge, access, relationships, or responsibilities exist with only one person.

This guide outlines a practical way to identify single points of failure and reduce dependency without creating unnecessary bureaucracy.

The goal is not to eliminate individual ownership.

The goal is to make sure important operations can continue when someone is unavailable.

---

# What Is a Single Point of Failure?

A single point of failure exists when one person, system, vendor, or process is so critical that its absence would significantly disrupt operations.

Examples include:

* Only one employee knows how a recurring process works
* One person controls an important company account
* Vendor relationships exist only in one person's inbox
* Only one employee has access to a system
* A key process is undocumented
* One vendor provides a critical service with no backup plan
* Important deadlines depend on someone's memory
* No one knows what to do when the primary owner is unavailable

Single points of failure may exist for years without causing a problem.

They become visible when something goes wrong.

# Common Small Team Risks

## Knowledge Risk

Critical knowledge exists only in one person's memory.

Examples:

* How payroll information is submitted
* How new employee accounts are created
* Which vendor handles a specific issue
* How a recurring report is produced
* How an office system is reset

## Access Risk

Only one person has the credentials or permissions needed to complete an important task.

Examples:

* Building administration
* Software administration
* Financial systems
* Shared company accounts
* Vendor portals

Access should always follow company security policies.

Reducing access risk does not mean sharing passwords informally.

It means using approved ownership, permissions, and recovery procedures.

## Vendor Risk

Only one person understands the vendor relationship.

They may be the only person who knows:

* The primary contact
* Contract terms
* Billing process
* Escalation procedure
* Renewal date
* Service history

## Process Risk

A recurring process depends entirely on one employee.

Examples:

* Employee onboarding
* Offboarding
* Purchasing
* Office opening
* Event coordination
* Budget reporting
* Facilities escalation

## System Risk

An organization depends on one tool without a clear backup or recovery process.

Examples:

* Shared files stored in one personal account
* No backup for operational data
* Important information stored in a platform nobody else understands
* No recovery owner for administrative accounts

## Decision Risk

Only one person has enough context to approve or move work forward.

This may create unnecessary delays when that person is unavailable.

# Step 1: Identify Critical Operations

Start by listing the processes that would create significant disruption if they stopped.

Examples:

| Process | Primary Owner | Business Impact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employee Onboarding | Operations | New hires cannot start successfully |
| Building Access | Office Manager | Employees cannot enter workspace |
| Payroll Coordination | People Operations | Employees may not be paid correctly |
| Vendor Payments | Finance | Services may be interrupted |
| IT Administration | IT Lead | Accounts and systems may become inaccessible |
| Facilities Escalation | Workplace Operations | Critical workplace issues may remain unresolved |

Focus first on high-impact processes.

Not every task requires backup coverage.

---

# Step 2: Ask the Bus Factor Question

The "bus factor" is a simple way to think about dependency.

Ask:

> If this person became unexpectedly unavailable tomorrow, could someone else complete this process?

Possible answers:

### Yes

The process is documented and another person can perform it.

### Maybe

Some information exists, but additional support would be required.

### No

The organization would be unable to continue the process without the primary owner.

Processes in the "No" category should receive priority.

---

# Step 3: Create a Risk Inventory

Build a simple tracker.

| Process | Primary Owner | Backup Owner | Documentation | Access Coverage | Risk Level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onboarding | Operations | HR | Complete | Complete | Low |
| Building Access | Office Manager | None | Partial | None | High |
| Vendor Management | Operations | Finance | Partial | Complete | Medium |
| Budget Reporting | Operations | Finance | Complete | Complete | Low |

This makes hidden dependencies visible.

---

# Step 4: Assign Backup Ownership

Critical processes should have backup coverage when practical.

A backup owner does not need to manage the process every day.

They should understand:

* What the process does
* Where documentation lives
* What systems are required
* Who the important contacts are
* When the process must happen
* How to escalate problems

The backup should be capable of keeping the process moving temporarily.

---

# Step 5: Document the Process

Documentation should be practical.

A backup owner should be able to understand:

1. What triggers the process
2. Who owns it
3. What systems are used
4. What information is required
5. What steps must happen
6. What deadlines apply
7. What common problems occur
8. Who can help when something goes wrong

For critical processes, documentation should be tested rather than assumed to be useful.

---

# Step 6: Document Important Contacts

Maintain shared contact information for important operational relationships.

Examples:

* Building management
* Property manager
* IT support
* Cleaning vendor
* Security provider
* Payroll provider
* Insurance broker
* Food service
* Equipment vendor
* Emergency maintenance

A vendor relationship should not disappear when one employee leaves.

---

# Step 7: Review Account Ownership

Company systems should not depend entirely on personal employee accounts.

Review:

* Administrative ownership
* Recovery methods
* Access permissions
* Shared accounts
* Billing ownership
* Vendor portals
* Software licenses

Questions to ask:

* Who is the current administrator?
* Is there an approved backup administrator?
* Can the company recover access if the owner leaves?
* Is ownership tied to a company-controlled account?
* Are permissions documented?

Always follow security policies and least-privilege principles.

---

# Step 8: Build a Coverage Plan

For high-risk processes, document what should happen when the primary owner is unavailable.

Example:

| Process | Primary Owner | Backup | Immediate Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Facilities Emergency | Workplace Lead | Operations Manager | Contact building management |
| New Hire Setup | Operations | HR Coordinator | Review onboarding tracker |
| Vendor Payment Issue | Finance | Operations | Contact Accounts Payable |
| IT Access Issue | IT Admin | Managed IT Vendor | Open priority support ticket |

The goal is continuity, not perfection.

---

# Step 9: Cross-Train Selectively

Cross-training every employee on every process is unnecessary.

Prioritize processes that are:

* High risk
* Time sensitive
* Frequently used
* Difficult to recover
* Important to employee safety or business continuity

Cross-training may include:

* Shadowing
* Process walkthroughs
* Practice scenarios
* Shared documentation review
* Temporary coverage

---

# Step 10: Test the Backup

A backup plan is not useful if nobody knows whether it works.

Periodically test critical processes.

Examples:

* Have the backup owner complete a routine task
* Ask someone unfamiliar with the process to follow the SOP
* Confirm backup administrator access
* Verify vendor contacts
* Review account recovery procedures
* Simulate coverage during planned time off

Any confusion identified during testing should improve the documentation.

# Risk Prioritization

Not every single point of failure deserves the same level of attention.

A simple framework can use:

## Impact

How disruptive would failure be?

### Low

Minor inconvenience.

### Medium

Noticeable operational disruption.

### High

Major employee, financial, security, or business impact.

## Likelihood

How likely is the dependency to become a problem?

### Low

Rarely used or easily recovered.

### Medium

Regular dependency with some alternatives.

### High

Frequently used with no backup.

## Example Risk Matrix

| Process | Impact | Likelihood | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Office Supply Ordering | Low | Medium | Low |
| Building Access | High | Medium | High |
| Payroll Coordination | High | Low | High |
| Event Ordering | Medium | Medium | Medium |
| Shared Drive Administration | High | High | Critical |

# Warning Signs

A team may have excessive single-person dependency when employees frequently say:

* "Only Alex knows how to do that."
* "That password is on someone's laptop."
* "I think Jordan has the vendor contact."
* "We have to wait until they get back."
* "Nobody knows when the contract renews."
* "That process isn't written down."
* "We've always just asked them."
* "I'm not sure who owns that."

These statements are useful signals.

They show where operational resilience may be weak.

---

# Avoiding Overcorrection

Reducing operational risk does not require creating a backup for every minor responsibility.

Avoid:

* Giving unnecessary system access
* Creating excessive documentation
* Assigning several owners to simple tasks
* Duplicating work
* Building complicated contingency plans for low-risk activities
* Creating process purely because something could theoretically go wrong

The amount of backup should match the level of risk.

# Example Scenario

## Situation

A 25-person company relies on one Operations Manager for building access, vendor relationships, office purchasing, onboarding coordination, and several software accounts.

The Operations Manager plans to take two weeks of leave.

## Risk Review

The company discovers:

* No backup administrator exists for the building access system
* Vendor contacts live primarily in the manager's email
* Onboarding procedures are partially documented
* Office purchasing can be handled by Finance
* Several routine processes already have clear documentation

## Response

The team:

1. Assigns a backup administrator for building access.
2. Creates a shared vendor directory.
3. Updates the onboarding checklist.
4. Documents escalation contacts.
5. Confirms Finance can provide purchasing coverage.
6. Conducts a process walkthrough with the backup owner.

## Result

The Operations Manager can take leave without routine operations depending entirely on their availability.

The company also gains stronger long-term continuity.

# Quarterly Single Point of Failure Review

Small teams can periodically review:

## People

* Has anyone become the sole owner of a critical process?
* Do important responsibilities have backup coverage?

## Systems

* Are administrative accounts properly owned?
* Are recovery methods current?

## Vendors

* Are vendor contacts and contract details documented?

## Knowledge

* Are critical procedures current?
* Could another employee follow them?

## Upcoming Changes

* Is someone taking extended leave?
* Is an employee leaving?
* Is ownership changing?
* Is the company introducing a critical new system?

- [ ] Critical processes identified
- [ ] Primary owners documented
- [ ] High-risk processes have backups
- [ ] Critical procedures documented
- [ ] Important vendor information shared appropriately
- [ ] Administrative account ownership reviewed
- [ ] Recovery procedures confirmed
- [ ] Backup owners understand responsibilities
- [ ] Critical coverage tested
- [ ] Risks reviewed periodically

---

# Key Takeaway

Strong operations should not depend on one person being available at all times.

Small teams can reduce operational risk without adding unnecessary bureaucracy by identifying critical dependencies, documenting important knowledge, creating appropriate backup ownership, and testing whether coverage actually works.

The goal is not to make every employee interchangeable.

The goal is to make the organization resilient.
