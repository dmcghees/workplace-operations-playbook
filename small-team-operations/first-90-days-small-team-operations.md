# First 90 Days: Building Operations for a Small Team

## Purpose

Building operations for a small or growing organization does not require fixing everything at once.

The first 90 days should focus on understanding how the organization works, identifying operational risk, creating visibility, and introducing simple systems where they will have the greatest impact.

The goal is to build a reliable operating foundation without creating unnecessary bureaucracy.

---

# First 30 Days: Understand Before Changing

The first month should focus primarily on learning.

Before redesigning processes, understand how work currently gets done.

## 1. Learn the Organization

Understand:

* Team structure
* Leadership priorities
* Current headcount
* Hiring plans
* Work locations
* Remote or hybrid expectations
* Major vendors
* Current tools
* Important deadlines
* Active projects
* Known operational problems

## 2. Meet Key Stakeholders

Potential stakeholders may include:

* Leadership
* Finance
* Human Resources
* Recruiting
* Information Technology
* Workplace or Facilities
* Department managers
* External vendors

Ask questions such as:

* What is working well?
* What regularly creates problems?
* What takes too much time?
* What depends heavily on one person?
* What do employees frequently ask about?
* Which deadlines are easy to miss?
* What information is difficult to find?
* What upcoming changes should Operations prepare for?

## 3. Map Existing Processes

Document how important processes currently work.

Examples:

* Onboarding
* Offboarding
* Purchasing
* Vendor management
* Employee requests
* Facilities support
* Events
* Recruiting coordination
* Budget approvals
* Technology requests

Do not assume that the official process and the actual process are the same.

## 4. Identify Operational Risk

Look for areas where failure would create significant disruption.

Examples:

* Only one person knows how a critical process works
* Important contracts are not tracked
* Building access information is undocumented
* Vendor relationships exist only in personal inboxes
* Employee onboarding depends on memory
* Company accounts have unclear ownership
* Purchases occur without visibility
* Important requests are communicated verbally

## 5. Create an Operations Inventory

Build a list of what Operations currently owns or supports.

| Area | Current Owner | Current Process | Risk | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onboarding | [Owner] | Email checklist | Medium | High |
| Vendor Management | [Owner] | Individual inbox | High | High |
| Office Supplies | [Owner] | Informal ordering | Low | Medium |
| Events | [Owner] | Ad hoc | Medium | Medium |
| Budget Tracking | [Owner] | Spreadsheet | Medium | High |

This creates a baseline before changes are introduced.

# Days 31–60: Create Visibility and Consistency

Once the current environment is understood, begin introducing lightweight structure.

## 1. Establish One Operations Request Channel

Employees should know where to ask for help.

Possible options include:

* Shared inbox
* Request form
* Ticketing system
* Dedicated internal channel

The specific tool matters less than having a clear entry point.

## 2. Build an Operations Dashboard

Track major operational priorities such as:

* Open employee requests
* New hires
* Departures
* Facilities issues
* Vendor issues
* Upcoming events
* Purchases
* Projects
* Risks

The dashboard should answer:

**What needs attention right now?**

## 3. Create an Onboarding Process

Start with a simple checklist.

At minimum, track:

* Employee information
* Start date
* Equipment
* Accounts
* Workplace access
* Manager readiness
* First day logistics

The goal is to identify missing items before the employee starts.

## 4. Create an Offboarding Process

Track:

* Equipment return
* Access removal coordination
* Keys or badges
* Workspace cleanup
* Knowledge transfer
* Ownership changes

Offboarding should not depend on someone remembering each step.

## 5. Build a Vendor Directory

Document:

* Vendor
* Service
* Contact
* Internal owner
* Cost
* Contract dates
* Renewal dates
* Escalation contact

This reduces dependency on personal inboxes and institutional memory.

## 6. Create Basic Budget Visibility

At minimum, track:

* Vendor expenses
* Workplace expenses
* Software
* Events
* Supplies
* Upcoming renewals
* Planned purchases

The goal is not to replace Finance.

The goal is for Operations to understand the cost of the services it manages.

## 7. Document Critical Procedures

Start with procedures that are:

* High risk
* Frequently used
* Difficult to explain
* Dependent on one person

Examples:

* Building access
* New hire setup
* Vendor escalation
* Emergency contacts
* Office opening and closing
* Purchasing approval

Do not try to document everything.

# Days 61–90: Improve and Prepare to Scale

The final phase should focus on improving the systems created during the first two months.

## 1. Review Recurring Problems

Look for patterns.

Examples:

* Equipment frequently arrives late
* Employees do not know where to submit requests
* Vendor invoices are incorrect
* Supplies repeatedly run out
* Interview scheduling is slow
* Events require last-minute purchases

Recurring problems are opportunities for process improvement.

## 2. Define Service Expectations

Clarify what employees can expect from Operations.

Examples:

* Requests acknowledged within one business day
* Urgent facilities issues escalated immediately
* New hire requests submitted at least ten business days before start
* Event requests submitted by an established deadline

Expectations help employees plan and help Operations prioritize.

## 3. Establish Backup Ownership

Critical processes should not stop because one person is unavailable.

Identify backup coverage for:

* Building access
* Key vendors
* Employee onboarding
* Purchasing
* Technology administration
* Payroll coordination
* Emergency contacts

## 4. Review Tools

Ask whether current systems are still appropriate.

Questions include:

* Are employees using the request channel?
* Is the tracker becoming difficult to maintain?
* Are duplicate systems being used?
* Is manual work creating unnecessary errors?
* Would automation meaningfully reduce repetitive work?

Only introduce new technology when it solves a real problem.

## 5. Establish Basic KPIs

Choose a small number of useful measures.

Examples:

### Onboarding

Percentage of employees ready before Day One.

### Facilities

Requests resolved within target timeframe.

### Vendors

Recurring service issues.

### Inventory

Number of stockouts or rush orders.

### Budget

Actual spending compared with forecast.

### Employee Requests

Average response or resolution time.

Five useful metrics are better than thirty metrics nobody uses.

## 6. Create a Regular Operations Review

A weekly review may cover:

* Current priorities
* Open risks
* Upcoming hires
* Vendor issues
* Budget concerns
* Events
* Projects
* Leadership decisions needed

A monthly review may focus more on:

* Trends
* Spending
* KPIs
* Vendor performance
* Process improvements
* Upcoming risks

## 7. Build a Six Month Roadmap

At the end of 90 days, identify what should happen next.

Possible priorities include:

* Implementing a ticketing system
* Improving vendor contracts
* Automating onboarding
* Developing stronger reporting
* Creating additional SOPs
* Consolidating software
* Improving purchasing controls
* Planning office growth
* Building employee experience programs

# What Not to Do in the First 90 Days

Avoid:

* Replacing every existing tool immediately
* Writing SOPs for every task
* Creating approval requirements without a clear reason
* Introducing complicated systems before understanding the problem
* Assuming informal processes are automatically bad
* Changing workflows without talking to the people who use them
* Measuring activity simply because it can be measured

The first 90 days should create clarity, not disruption.

# Example Scenario

## Situation

An Operations Manager joins a 30-person growing company.

The company has strong employees but limited operational structure.

Onboarding is handled through email, vendors are managed independently, employee requests arrive through chat, and spending information is scattered.

## First 30 Days

The Operations Manager:

* Meets department leaders
* Maps existing workflows
* Creates an operations responsibility inventory
* Reviews vendors and contracts
* Identifies critical operational risks
* Documents upcoming hiring and projects

## Days 31–60

The Operations Manager:

* Introduces a shared operations request channel
* Creates onboarding and offboarding checklists
* Builds a vendor directory
* Creates a basic budget tracker
* Establishes a weekly operations dashboard
* Documents several critical SOPs

## Days 61–90

The Operations Manager:

* Reviews recurring issues
* Establishes service expectations
* Creates backup ownership for critical processes
* Introduces several operational KPIs
* Identifies opportunities for automation
* Presents a six-month operations roadmap to leadership

## Result

The organization gains greater visibility, clearer ownership, and more consistent processes while maintaining the flexibility of a small team.

# 90-Day Success Questions

At the end of the first 90 days, ask:

* Do employees know where to request operational support?
* Are major responsibilities clearly owned?
* Are critical processes documented?
* Are vendor relationships visible?
* Is onboarding more predictable?
* Can leadership understand operational priorities?
* Is spending easier to track?
* Are recurring issues being identified?
* Do important processes have backup coverage?
* Is there a clear roadmap for what Operations should improve next?

If the answer to most of these questions is yes, the foundation is working.

# Key Takeaway

The first 90 days in a small organization should not be about building the most sophisticated operations function possible.

They should be about understanding the organization, reducing risk, creating visibility, clarifying ownership, and introducing simple systems that solve real problems.

The strongest operations functions grow with the organization rather than getting ahead of it.
