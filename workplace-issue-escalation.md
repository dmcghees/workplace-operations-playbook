# Workplace Issue Escalation

## Purpose

This guide outlines a practical approach for escalating workplace operations issues.

The goal is to resolve problems at the appropriate level while making sure urgent, sensitive, or high impact issues reach the correct person quickly.

## When to Escalate

A workplace issue may need escalation when:

* There is a safety concern
* Security may be affected
* Multiple employees are impacted
* Building access is unavailable
* A facilities problem could cause property damage
* A vendor has not resolved a recurring issue
* Additional spending approval is required
* The issue involves confidential information
* The problem is outside the authority of Workplace Operations
* Normal troubleshooting has not resolved the issue

## Assess the Situation

Before escalating, determine:

* What happened?
* Where is the issue occurring?
* Who is affected?
* How many people are affected?
* Is there an immediate safety concern?
* Is there a temporary workaround?
* What has already been attempted?
* What happens if the issue is not resolved quickly?

This information helps determine urgency.

## Example Priority Levels

### Urgent

Issues requiring immediate attention may include:

* Water leak
* Electrical hazard
* Security breach
* Loss of building access
* Fire or life safety issue
* Major workplace outage

Follow emergency or safety procedures immediately when appropriate.

### High Priority

Examples may include:

* Large area without heating or cooling
* Multiple employees unable to access a workplace system
* Critical conference room failure before an executive meeting
* Major vendor service failure

### Standard Priority

Examples may include:

* Furniture repair
* Minor maintenance request
* Noncritical supply issue
* Cosmetic damage

## Escalation Process

### 1. Document the Issue

Record:

* Location
* Time reported
* Description
* Employees affected
* Business impact
* Troubleshooting completed
* Photos when useful and appropriate

### 2. Contact the Correct Resource

Depending on the issue, this may include:

* Building management
* Security
* Information technology
* Human Resources
* Workplace leadership
* Property management
* Vendor support
* Finance
* Senior management

Avoid sending an issue broadly when there is a clear owner.

### 3. Explain the Business Impact

Instead of only describing the technical problem, explain how it affects employees or operations.

For example:

"Conference room display is not working."

is less useful than:

"Conference room display is not working and a 20 person client meeting begins in 30 minutes."

### 4. Provide Actions Already Taken

Include what has already been attempted so the next person does not repeat the same work.

### 5. Track the Escalation

Document:

* Person or team contacted
* Time escalated
* Ticket or work order number
* Expected next step
* Estimated response time when available

### 6. Communicate Updates

Keep affected employees informed when appropriate.

A useful update may include:

* What is affected
* What is being done
* Available workaround
* When another update will be provided

Avoid promising a resolution time that has not been confirmed.

## Example Escalation

**Issue:** Main office entry badge reader stopped recognizing employee badges.

**Scope:** Multiple employees unable to enter the office without assistance.

**Initial troubleshooting:** Tested several active badges and confirmed the issue affected the reader rather than an individual badge.

**Business impact:** Employees could not independently enter the workplace.

**Action taken:** Contacted building security and property management, arranged temporary access procedures, and notified employees of the temporary process.

**Follow up:** Tracked the repair request and confirmed normal badge access after service was completed.

## Vendor Escalation

Escalation may also be necessary when a vendor:

* Misses scheduled service repeatedly
* Does not respond within expected timeframes
* Provides incomplete work
* Creates recurring service issues
* Charges unexpected costs
* Fails to meet agreed service expectations

Document recurring issues before discussing vendor performance with the contract owner or workplace leadership.

## Closing the Issue

Before considering an escalation complete:

1. Confirm the issue has been resolved.
2. Notify affected employees when appropriate.
3. Close the related ticket or work order.
4. Document the final resolution.
5. Identify whether the incident suggests a process improvement.
6. Update an SOP or checklist if the same problem could happen again.

## Key Takeaway

Effective escalation is not simply forwarding a problem to someone else.

It means identifying the right owner, communicating the impact clearly, documenting what has already been done, and staying involved until the issue reaches resolution.
